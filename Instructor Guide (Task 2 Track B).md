# Instructor Guide · Task 2 · Track B

## Overview

Track B produces a redesign specification rather than a working modification. Where Track A's pedagogical force comes from making students implement and feel the consequences of design decisions, Track B's force comes from making them commit, in writing, to a normative position and defend it under a constrained word count.

The single hardest move for students is the framework choice the case demands - Scotia's Data Ethics Commitment, the EU AI Act, AIDA, or a new community-centered framework. Most students will instinctively hedge, list options, and avoid committing. This task is designed specifically to surface that instinct and force students past it.

**Recommended for:** courses focused on AI ethics, policy, organizational design, or governance where students may not have access to AI-assisted coding tools, or where the curriculum prioritizes normative reasoning over implementation.
**Session length:** 75–90 minutes (can be split across two sessions, with the first focused on critique and the second on synthesis).
**Class size:** 15–60 students; individually or in teams of 2–3.
**Prerequisites:** Students must have read the case (especially Appendices B and C) and completed Task 1.

## Session Structure

### Phase 1 · Orientation and Diagnosis (20 min)
- Briefly walk through the working prototype on screen so students see what they are redesigning. Emphasize that they will not modify it.
- Anchor the session in Julian's question: *"what should come next for the Ethics Assistant?"*
- Ask students to name the two or three governance gaps in the current tool they consider most consequential. Capture these on a board. The set typically converges on: GenAI blindness, the self-reporting vulnerability, the disconnect between a clean score and unresolved flags, and the absence of affected-community context.

### Phase 2 · The Framework Choice (20 min)
- Place the four anchoring frameworks on the board: Scotia's Data Ethics Commitment, the EU AI Act, AIDA, and a new community-centered framework.
- Ask each student or team to commit to one silently, on paper or a slide *before* discussing.
- Solicit votes and ask three or four students with different choices to defend theirs in 90 seconds. The instructor's job is to press: *what does your choice rule out? what does it protect against?*
- This phase models what the deliverable requires. Students who watch a peer commit and defend a choice are more able to do it themselves on paper.

### Phase 3 · Drafting (30–40 min)
- Students draft the proposal individually or in teams. Circulate. Watch for:
  - **Aspirational vagueness.** "Improve fairness monitoring" - push for the specific mechanism.
  - **Option-cataloguing.** Multiple framework candidates discussed without commitment - push for the choice.
  - **Disengagement from the case.** Generic AI ethics rhetoric with no AMCA, no six flags, no Julian - push back to the document.
  - **Trade-off blindness.** Proposals that claim improvement without naming costs - push for what the move sacrifices.

### Phase 4 · Peer Critique and Debrief (15–20 min, optional)
- Pair teams and have each give the other a five-minute critique focused on a single question: *"Where in this proposal did the writer dodge a hard choice?"*
- Whole-class debrief on the framework choice: what did students learn from having to commit? Did anyone's choice change after they tried to defend it?

### Suggested Debrief Questions
- *"What does the EU AI Act anchor protect against that Scotia's Data Ethics Commitment does not and vice versa?"*
- *"If you centred Indigenous customers, unbanked individuals, and Mexican retail customers in the framework, what changes in the assessment? What gets harder?"*
- *"Where in your proposal did you make a hard choice? Where did you avoid one?"*

## Common Failure Modes to Pre-empt

1. **The cataloguing proposal.** *"There are several options for the framework anchor. Each has merits..."* followed by no choice. Tell students at the outset: this writing pattern is treated as a non-deliverable. The case explicitly forbids it.

2. **The all-purpose redesign.** Proposes ten changes, executes none. The 1,000-word limit is a feature; it forces selectivity. Encourage two or three deeply specified moves over a wide menu of gestures.

3. **The detached proposal.** Treats the assignment as a generic AI ethics essay, never references AMCA, the six flags, the human-oversight mischaracterization, or Julian. The rubric heavily weights case grounding.

4. **The technocratic dodge.** Proposes more automated detection without confronting the trade-off (false positives, mischaracterized flags, disengagement of submitters who feel the tool is policing them rather than helping). Name this trade-off as a pattern in the debrief.

## Assessment Rubric

| Criterion | Excellent (A) | Good (B) | Adequate (C) | Insufficient |
|-----------|---------------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| **Diagnostic fidelity** | Identifies the most material gaps; reasoning visibly draws from the case. | Solid diagnosis with minor omissions. | Generic gaps; case is gestured at, not engaged. | Off-topic or based on misreading the case. |
| **Specificity** | Concrete, implementable moves with named mechanisms. | Some specifics, some aspiration. | Mostly aspirational. | Slogans. |
| **Normative commitment** | Makes a clear framework choice and defends it, including what it rules out. | Commits but defence is thin. | Hedges; commits weakly. | Lists options without committing. |
| **Trade-off honesty** | Names costs of the proposed moves explicitly and credibly. | Acknowledges some trade-offs. | Glancing acknowledgement. | Treats the proposal as costless. |
| **Case grounding** | AMCA, six flags, Julian's framing, communities, all engaged with substance. | Most case touchpoints engaged. | Surface references only. | Not visibly drawn from the case. |
